They told me the right letter. Nobody told me why I was wrong.

I have spent about 20 years in quantitative finance, and I have a Master of Science in Computational Finance from Carnegie Mellon University. None of that makes the Series 65 easy. It is mostly law, ethics and definitions, and the exam wants the answer the rules give, not the one that works in practice.

Over about two months I took 10 full practice tests, roughly 1,900 questions, and averaged about 80% on my last six. My prep course’s question bank was hard in an awkward way: long question stems, Roman-numeral formats and gotchas. Alongside it I kept my own cram sheets: for every rule I kept missing, I wrote down the trap, why the wrong answer looks right, and the rule that settles it, with a link to the source. I wrote my practice questions from the official outline the way the real exam asks them: short stems and two or three close, believable answer choices.

What the real exam was like

The first 25 or so questions were easy. Then they got noticeably harder. I could usually narrow a question down to two choices, but both sounded right, and the official wording made the wrong one sound perfectly plausible. That lasted until about question 115. Somewhere in the middle I was sure I was failing. Around question 86 I told myself: “I won’t give up. If I fail, I’ll take it again. Just focus on this test.” The last 25 felt easier. I couldn’t tell which 10 questions were the unscored ones.

I never went back to the questions I had marked for review. If you have trained for it, trust your instincts and don’t second-guess yourself. I finished in an hour and a half, and I was honestly surprised when I passed.

Unlike my prep course’s question bank, the real exam had no Roman-numeral questions, and the stems were short. Almost every question came down to two close answers, and the wrong one was worded so realistically it could have been right. It tests whether you understand the rules well enough to apply them. Knowing the material gets you to two choices. Knowing why the believable one is wrong gets you the point.

This app simulates that. Short questions, no Roman numerals, and two close, believable answers on almost every question, the way the real exam felt. When you pick the wrong one, it tells you exactly why it looked right and the rule that settles it.
